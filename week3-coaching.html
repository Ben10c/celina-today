Sitting down with Celina

Three evenings, twenty minutes each, and a different job for you in each one. Tuesday you interview her. Thursday you keep your mouth shut. Friday you can finally just tell her the answer.

Tue · 20 min Pre-learning Grassland vs rainforest Thu · 20 min Maths Column addition Fri · 20 min Spelling Prefixes mis- un- in- dis-

Before any of it

Five things that apply to all three evenings. The first one is the one you will want to break.

  1. Set a visible 20-minute timer, and stop when it goes — even mid-question. This is the school's instruction, not a suggestion.
  2. The pencil stays in her hand. Including the ruled line, including the title.
  3. Don't rub out her mistakes. Mr Barron needs to see them. A wrong answer that shows her method is worth more to him than a right one you supplied.
  4. If it was hard, write a note in the book. That is the school's own channel — "Stopped at 20 min. Carrying into the hundreds still shaky." Nothing more elaborate than that.
  5. Pencil and ruler; week and task as a title. Maths goes on the squared paper.

"Home learning should fit within the allocated time — there is no need to continue for significantly longer."

Year 4 Home Learning Guidelines

A stamp in the book later means the task was discussed in class and she has had verbal feedback. That is what you are looking for, not a mark.

Tuesday

Two biomes, compared

Your role: interviewer. Ask, don't tell.

A pre-learning task exists so that she walks into the Humanities lesson with something to say. If you explain biomes to her tonight, you have spent the lesson's ammunition before she gets there. Your job is to get ideas out of her, not put them in.

0–5 minShe reads both texts aloud
5–13 minYou talk. Nothing written yet
13–20 minShe fills the frame

Say this

  • "Read me the grassland one. Now the rainforest one."
  • "Which one would be easier to walk through? Why?"
  • "Both of them are hot. So what actually makes them different?"Steer to water. Everything else follows from it.
  • "The rainforest has layers. Why doesn't the grassland?"Trees are far apart — there's nothing to climb.
  • "So if the trees are far apart, what do the animals have to be good at?"Running, travelling a long way, reaching up.
  • "Why does the giraffe need a long neck and the monkey doesn't?"

The thing worth landing

The water decides the plants, and the plants decide the animals. Rain all year → dense trees in layers → animals that climb and hide. A dry season → grass and scattered trees → animals that run, graze and migrate.

If she says a version of that back to you, stop pushing — she is ready for the lesson. That causal chain is exactly what the Humanities unit is building towards.

Watch for

Listing animals. A list is not a comparison, and it is what most of the class will hand in. Push every observation one step further: "and what does that tell you?"

An empty middle column. Children fill the two outer columns easily and leave "what is the same" thin. Prompt for it — both hot, both tropical, both have trees, both have rain at some point in the year, both have animals that hide or camouflage, both have plants and animals shaped by their climate.

If she's stuck

The frame already has two answers filled in for her: dry seasons and wet all year. Give those back to her and ask what follows from each one.

The closing question

The frame ends with "how might plants and animals have adapted to the biome?" — that is the one the teacher will actually pick up on. Three she can get to on her own: the giraffe's neck, the lion's fur in dry grass, and the rainforest fungi that break down dead leaves on the floor.

Thursday

Column addition

Your role: referee. Watch the method, not the answer.

Ask her first: which task did the maths teacher say to do — 1, 2 or 3? The booklet says the teacher tells the child, and the sheet itself doesn't say. Task 1 is two-digit with no carrying at all and is well below where she should be; Task 3 is the one that teaches exchange.

If she genuinely doesn't know, do Task 3 and write that in the book.

0–3 minRule the page, write the title
3–17 minTen sums, estimate before each
17–20 minCheck by subtraction

Set-up counts as much as the answers

  • One digit per square. Units under units — that is the whole game.
  • Rule the line under the sum with the ruler, not freehand.
  • The exchanged digit goes small, underneath the next column along.
  • Title it Week 3 — Maths Task 3 so the teacher knows which she was set.

Say this

  • "Estimate first. Roughly what should this come to?"397 + 455 is about 400 + 450, so about 850. Actual: 852.
  • "There are 12 ones, so I do need to make an exchange."
  • "I can exchange 10 ones for 1 ten."
  • "Now check it. 852 take away 455 should give you back 397."

The middle two are lifted straight from the White Rose calculation policy Tanglin teaches from — they are the words she hears in class. Say exchange, never "carry" or "borrow". A competing word costs her.

Source: White Rose Year 4 policy · addition

The four errors to watch for

  1. Lining the numbers up on the left. Task 2 is full of mixed-width sums — 583 + 31, 679 + 25. The 31 must sit under the 83, not under the 58. This is the single most common mistake at this stage.
  2. Writing the exchanged 1 and then not adding it in.
  3. Putting the exchanged 1 in the wrong column.
  4. Assuming the answer has three digits. Two sums in Task 3 break past a thousand: 627 + 891 = 1518 and 753 + 918 = 1671. Warn her that an answer is allowed to grow a column.

If she finishes early

Skip the missing-digit questions and go straight to the digit-swap investigation at the bottom of the optional page. It is the only part with a genuine discovery in it, and it takes about ten minutes.

  • "Q10 is done for you — 782 became 980. That's up by 198. Now do 11 and 12."627 + 891 = 1518 → 726 + 891 = 1617, up 99.   289 + 667 = 956 → 982 + 667 = 1649, up 693.
  • "198, 99 and 693. What do all three have in common?"All multiples of 99. Let her find it — don't say it.
  • "Could it ever go down instead?"Yes. Swap 753 to 357 and the total drops by 396.

The rule underneath it: swapping the hundreds and units digits changes the total by (units − hundreds) × 99. Up when the units digit is bigger, down when it's smaller, unchanged when they match. So the answer to Q13 is no — not always, which is what the question is fishing for.

Friday

Prefixes

Your role: quizmaster. Here you can just tell her.

The lightest of the three, and the only one where supplying the answer does no harm. She must do one task from "Stick it!" (the syllable puzzle) and one from "Store it!" (the alphabetical sort). The stretch task is genuinely optional.

0–4 minThe rule, and the three traps
4–12 minPattern Puzzle — syllables
12–20 minSpeed Sort — alphabetical

The rule, in one sentence

A prefix is glued to the front of a whole word and changes its meaning — the root word never loses a letter. That one sentence prevents most of the errors in this list.

Test it on the three traps in her word list

  • disappoint = dis + appoint. Two p's, because appoint has them. One s. Not "dissapoint".
  • incorrect = in + correct. Two r's, because correct has them.
  • independent = in + dependent.
  • "Cover the prefix. What's the word underneath? Spell that. Now stick the prefix back on."

Tell her plainly: there is no rule for which "not" prefix a word takes. un-, in- and dis- all mean not, and which one goes with which word is memory, not logic. Say so, or she will waste the evening hunting for a pattern that isn't there. Only mis- carries its own distinct meaning — wrongly, or badly.

Pattern Puzzle — clap the syllables

Clapping beats counting in her head. All thirteen: mistake 2 · misuse 2 · misbehave 3 · unhappy 3 · unlock 2 · unsafe 2 · incorrect 3 · incapable 4 · independent 4 · disagree 3 · dislike 2 · dishonest 3 · disappoint 3.

Speed Sort — make her pick the hard six

If she chooses six easy words this takes two minutes and teaches nothing. Make her include all four dis- words: disagree, disappoint, dishonest, dislike. Now she has to read to the fourth letter to order them, which is the actual skill being tested.

If she wants the stretch

Word Family Expansion just needs the root, then everything that grows from it. The roots in her list: behave · take · happy · lock · agree · honest · correct · depend · appoint. Start her on behave — behave, behaved, behaving, behaviour, behavioural, misbehaviour. It's the richest one.

And every day, the reading

Twenty minutes aloud with you, banded book, logged in the diary and signed. It sits outside the sixty minutes of set work and it is the part the school cares about most. Her own bedtime book is separate — not logged, not corrected, not part of the twenty.
