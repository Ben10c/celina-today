Tuesday

Step inside a picture

Your role: interviewer. Ask, don't tell.

An English task this week. She picks one of four settings — a misty marsh forest, a sunlit green forest, an underwater coral city, or a land of fiery volcanoes — and builds a word bank for it in five columns: nouns, powerful adjectives, powerful verbs, similes, and a challenge column of expanded noun phrases.

The bank is raw material for a setting description she will write in class. The school's example is a child stepping through a glowing portal into a forest — the same image as The Land of Roar, the class novel. So this almost certainly feeds a description of the world beyond the portal. Words she generates tonight are words she gets to use in that lesson; words you supply are not hers.

0–3 minShe picks a picture and just looks
3–10 minYou talk. Five senses. Nothing written
10–20 minShe fills the columns

Let her choose — but know the trade

The volcano gives the strongest verbs (erupts, spits, churns, rumbles) and the easiest similes. The misty marsh is closest to the school's own example and the novel. The coral city is the hardest to find verbs for — everything just floats. Don't steer; if she picks the coral city, spend the talking time on what the water and the fish are doing.

Say this

  • "Look at it for a whole minute without talking. Now tell me five things you can see."Nouns column. Push each one towards precise: not tree but trunk, canopy, roots, branches; not water but lagoon, ripples, mist.
  • "Close your eyes. You've stepped in. What's the first thing you hear?"
  • "What's under your feet? What does the air smell of?"
  • "The mist — what is it doing?"Verbs. Curling, creeping, hovering, drifting, clinging. If she says "it's there", ask again.
  • "The trees are big. Big like what?"Adjectives. Towering, immense, ancient, gnarled. Ban big, scary, nice, pretty for the evening.
  • "What does the mist remind you of?"Simile. Must be something that is not mist — ghostly fingers, a grey blanket, smoke from a bonfire.

The thing worth landing

A word bank is only useful if the words are precise. Four strong words a column beat ten weak ones. The school's example has five nouns, five adjectives, five verbs, three adverbs and two similes — that is the scale, not a wall of words.

Watch for

"Is" and "are" in the verbs column. The mist is grey has no action in it. Every verb should answer "what is it doing?"

Similes that don't compare. "The forest was like a forest" and "the water was like the sea" both turn up. The second half must be a different thing.

Skipping the challenge column. An expanded noun phrase is two adjectives, a noun, and a "with" tail: the thick, ghostly mist · towering trees with tangled branches · rivers of glowing, orange lava. She only needs two or three, but they are the column the teacher will pick up on because they are what a setting description is built from.

If she's stuck

Give her the school's own example bank to read, not to copy: portal, pathway, canopy, branches, mushrooms · enchanted, towering, tangled, shadowy, sparkling · twisted, shimmered, hovered, swayed, crept · silently, cautiously, mysteriously · "The branches reached out like crooked fingers." Then ask which of those would also be true of her picture, and which she needs to change.

Thursday

Column subtraction

Your role: referee. Watch the method, not the answer.

Ask her first: which task did the maths teacher say to do — 1, 2 or 3? The sheet says "choose your challenge"; the booklet says the teacher tells the child. Task 1 is two-digit with no exchange at all. Task 2 is where exchange starts. Task 3 is the real Year 4 content, including three sums that exchange across a zero.

If she genuinely doesn't know, do Task 3 and write that in the book.

0–3 minRule the page, write the title
3–17 minTen sums, estimate before each
17–20 minCheck by adding back

Set-up counts as much as the answers

  • One digit per square. Units under units. Task 2 mixes widths — 583 − 31, 352 − 16 — and the 31 must sit under the 83.
  • Bigger number on top. Always. It is subtraction, not a race to line things up.
  • Cross out the digit she exchanges from and write the new one small above it. The exchanged ten goes small in front of the units. That is exactly what the sheet's own worked example shows for 234 − 158.
  • Title it Week 4 — Maths Task 3 so the teacher knows which she was set.

Say this

  • "Estimate first. Roughly?"652 − 327 is about 650 − 325, so about 325. Actual: 325. 1,000 − 486 is about 500.
  • "Can I take 7 from 2? No. So I need to exchange 1 ten for 10 ones."
  • "Now check it. 325 add 327 should give you back 652."

Say exchange, never "borrow". Borrowing implies giving it back; the school's word is exchange and it is the word she hears in class.

Source: White Rose Year 4 policy · subtraction

The four errors to watch for

  1. Taking the small digit from the big one, whichever is on top. 72 − 38 becomes "8 take 2 is 6", answer 46 instead of 34. This is the subtraction error at this age, and the whole of Task 2 is built to catch it. If the answer's units digit is 6, this is what happened.
  2. Exchanging across a zero. 703 − 458, 902 − 667 and 1,000 − 486 all need it. She can't take a ten from the 0, so she goes to the hundreds first: 7 hundreds becomes 6 hundreds and 10 tens, then 10 tens becomes 9 tens and 10 ones. Two exchanges before she subtracts a single digit. Let her get stuck here for a minute before helping — this is the one thing this week actually teaches.
  3. Exchanging and then not reducing the digit she took from. The crossed-out 3 must become a 2.
  4. Exchanging when she didn't need to. 671 − 139 needs one exchange in the units and none in the tens. Every column is a fresh decision.

If she finishes early

The optional page. Q1–3 are one missing digit and go quickly; Q4–6 need her to work through the exchanges and are worth doing. Skip "make your own" unless she is enjoying it. The digit-swap investigation at the bottom is the interesting one and takes about ten minutes.

  • "548 take 283 is 265. Now swap the 5 and the 8: 845 take 283. Up or down, and by how much?"562. Up by 297.
  • "Try 283 take 158. Now swap 283 to 382."125 → 224. Up 99. Then a case where units is smaller than hundreds — 712 − 305 = 407, swap to 217 − 305 and it goes negative. So it can go down.
  • "297, 99, 198. What do they have in common?"Multiples of 99. Let her find it.

The rule underneath: swapping the hundreds and units digits of a number changes it by (units − hundreds) × 99, so the difference moves by the same amount — up when the units digit is bigger, down when it's smaller. Q11's answer is it depends on the numbers, and the change is always a multiple of 99.

The sheet contradicts itself on Q10. The instruction says "reverse the digits in both numbers"; the worked example only swaps the first one (548 → 845) and leaves 283 alone. Follow the example — it is what the question actually asks her to compute. Either reading gives "it depends" for Q11.

Friday

Prefix mis-, applied

Your role: quizmaster. Here you can just tell her.

Week two of the spelling cycle: last week was learn the pattern, this week is apply it in writing. Eight mis- words in the vault — mistake, misuse, misbehave, misfortune, mislead, mistreat, misjudge, misheard — and the task is to copy three sentences into the book and underline every vault word. The Morphology Detective stretch is optional.

0–3 minThe rule, and the two traps
3–15 minThree sentences, copied exactly
15–20 minBook shut. You dictate the eight

The rule, in one sentence

mis- means wrongly or badly, and it is glued to the front of a whole word — the root never loses a letter. Last week's un-, in-, dis- all meant "not"; this is the one prefix in the unit with its own meaning.

The two traps in her list

  • misheard = mis + heard. The root is hear, but the sentence needs the past tense, and the past tense of hear is heard, not "heared". The school chose this word on purpose.
  • misused in sentence 1 — the vault word is misuse, and adding -ed drops nothing: mis + use + d. Not "misussed".

And one to say out loud: mislead (present) becomes misled (past). Sentence 3 says "tried to mislead", so it stays mislead — but if she ever writes "misleaded", that's the fix.

Copying is the task — so copy properly

The sentences carry commas the school wants her to notice: "Although people often misjudge Leo, he would never…" and "but, by a stroke of misfortune, he became lost too." Every comma copied. Every capital. Then underline the vault words — there are eight in the three sentences, and misused counts as one of them. Dishonest in sentence 3 is last week's word; underlining it is fine.

Then dictate

  • "Book shut. I'll say eight words. Write each one."Any she gets wrong: "cover the mis-. Spell the word underneath. Now stick it back on."

If she wants the stretch

Morphology Detective: pick one vault word, split it into prefix and root, say what the root means, list two or three words from the same family, and use the word in a sentence. The school's own example uses misheard, so pick a different one. mistreat is the richest: treat → treats, treated, treatment, mistreatment.
